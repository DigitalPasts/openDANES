Computational methods are expanding steadily beyond cuneiform and the classical languages, and this month **Ancient Egyptian** stands out. Hieratic now has a [diagnostic dataset for sign recognition and detection](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-36033-5_26){:target="_blank"} from Deir el-Medina, while machine translation is moving across the language's whole history, from [hieroglyphic-to-German](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.6/){:target="_blank"} to a [new state-of-the-art for Coptic](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1803/){:target="_blank"}. Both papers also raise the question of data contamination and how far reported scores can be trusted.

The foundations of digital classics are expanding even further, among other things due to new OCR capabilities. The [Open Greek and Latin Project](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.575){:target="_blank"} now holds 41 million words of Classical Greek, and a [companion article](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.595){:target="_blank"} traces how Perseus data has been reused across treebanks, annotated texts, and named entity resources. New datasets on metre, recipes, and Latin–Greek alignment join a new issue of *Digital Classics Online*. All of this comes together in the newly published [*Oxford Handbook of Digital Classical Studies*](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780197835210.001.0001){:target="_blank"}, whose 63 chapters map a field that has clearly come of age.

The study of the ancient world is also becoming more visible in the wider disciplines DANES draws on. The [ACL 2026 proceedings](https://aclanthology.org/events/acl-2026/){:target="_blank"} include twelve papers on ancient languages and scripts, from Sumerian to Indus, and the [DH2026 Book of Abstracts](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21495909){:target="_blank"} has around twenty, along with a mini-conference on AI and ancient scripts. The calls for papers for NAACL/COLING 2027, ACL 2027, and DH2027 openly invite work on under-resourced languages and linguistic diversity, so this is a good moment to submit.

The rest of this newsletter includes new datasets which make archaeological, environmental, and linguistic evidence open for reuse, and the special mentions ask how AI is changing research practice itself and how the past can be documented and protected. Upcoming seminars, workshops, and training series offer ways to build these skills. The job openings show institutions investing in long-term data infrastructure for the field.

## Recent Academic Publications

The **[64th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL 2026)](https://aclanthology.org/events/acl-2026/){:target="_blank"}** took place in **July 2026** in **San Diego, California**, and its proceedings, including Findings and the co-located workshops, are now available open access in the ACL Anthology. Among more than 6,000 papers, the following contributions deal with the languages and scripts of the ancient Near East and Mediterranean:

* **[Data Contamination in Neural Hieroglyphic Translation: A Reproducibility Study](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.6/){:target="_blank"}, by Ammar Toutou, Abdelrahman Harb, and Christine Basta**, shows that a reported score of 61.5 BLEU for hieroglyphic-to-German translation was inflated by overlap between training and test data, and releases a decontaminated test set with corrected baselines of 30.9–39.2 BLEU.
* **[Draft, Verify, Restore: Self-Refining Historical Inscription Restoration with a Unified MLLM](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1254/){:target="_blank"}, by Yuyi Zhang, Junle Liu, Peirong Zhang, Jianliang Liu, Zhenhua Yang, and Lianwen Jin**, presents a multimodal large language model (MLLM) that localizes damage in inscription images and restores the illegible text through iterative self-refinement.
* **[Enabling Stroke-Level Structural Analysis of Hieroglyphic Scripts without Language-Specific Priors](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1383/){:target="_blank"}, by Fuwen Luo, Zihao Wan, Ziyue Wang, Yaluo Liu, Pau Tong Lin Xu, Xuanjia Qiao, Xiaolong Wang, Peng Li, and Yang Liu**, introduces a framework that converts images of logographic characters into stroke-level line-segment representations, tested mainly on Chinese and oracle bone script, with exploratory experiments on Egyptian hieroglyphs and cuneiform.
* **[Evaluating Latin and Ancient Greek Sentence Alignment through Parallel Sentence Mining](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.11/){:target="_blank"}, by Sebastian Reichbauer, Shu Okabe, and Alexander Fraser**, compares six language models on a synthetic benchmark for detecting translations and intertextuality between Latin and Ancient Greek.
* **[Lost in Translation? Exploring the Shift in Grammatical Gender from Latin to Occitan](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.26/){:target="_blank"}, by Ahan Chatterjee, Matthias Schöffel, Matthias Aßenmacher, Marinus Wiedner, and Esteban Garces Arias**, uses an interpretable deep learning framework with a custom tokenizer to study the loss of the neuter gender between Latin and Occitan.
* **[Modeling the "Dalet" Clitic in Historical Hebrew Texts: A New Prefix-Segmented BERT Model and Stylistic Analysis](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.12/){:target="_blank"}, by Rachel Tal, Cheyn Shmuel Shmidman, and Avi Shmidman**, introduces a prefix-segmented BERT model that distinguishes the grammatical functions of the Aramaic proclitic dalet in a corpus of more than 300 million words of historical Hebrew.
* **[Statistical Structure in Indus Sign Sequences](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.28/){:target="_blank"}, by Tanishk Tiwari**, combines visual clustering, entropy-based analysis, and neural sequence modeling on 6,579 inscriptions to show that sign sequences in the undeciphered Indus script are directional and structured rather than random.
* **[StoicLLM: Preference Optimization for Philosophical Alignment in Small Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.32/){:target="_blank"}, by Ishmam Khan, Sindhuja Thogarrati, and Shuo Zhang**, fine-tunes small language models on foundational Stoic texts and evaluates how well they internalize Stoic virtues and duties.
* **[Syntax as a Rosetta Stone: Universal Dependencies for In-Context Coptic Translation](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1803/){:target="_blank"}, by Abhishek Purushothama, Emma Thronson, Alexia Guo, and Amir Zeldes**, combines retrieved dictionary glosses with Universal Dependencies parses in prompts to set a new state-of-the-art in Coptic machine translation.
* **[TabletCraft: Bridging a 4,000-Year Cultural Gap with Bidirectional Akkadian NMT and Cuneiform Rendering](https://aclanthology.org/2026.c3nlp-1.10/){:target="_blank"}, by Zhaohui Geoffrey Wang**, presents an open-source toolkit for translating between Akkadian and English in both directions, with a converter that renders English messages as cuneiform clay tablets.
* **[Translation or Recitation? Calibrating Evaluation Scores for Machine Translation of Extremely Low-Resource Languages](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-short.29/){:target="_blank"}, by Danlu Chen, Ka Sing He, Jiahe Tian, Chenghao Xiao, Zhaofeng Wu, Taylor Berg-Kirkpatrick, and Freda Shi**, proposes dataset-intrinsic metrics for contextualizing translation scores for extremely low-resource languages, including Akkadian and Ancient Egyptian, showing that much of the variation in reported results comes from train–test overlap and exposure during pretraining.
* **[Validator-Guided Hard Negative Mining for Masked Language Modeling in Low-Resource Ancient Languages](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-srw.69/){:target="_blank"}, by Andrei Voinea**, improves masked language modeling for Sumerian by combining a rule-based linguistic validator with hard negative mining, raising hit@10 accuracy from 18.0% to 86.7%.

Five new articles in [**Digital Classics Online**](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/dco){:target="_blank"} (volume 12, issue 1, 2026) apply computational methods to Greek and Roman sources. Several of the contributions discuss making interpretive criteria explicit and reproducible in digital workflows.

* **[Cacata carta digitalis. Modelling poetic shitstorm retrieval in a corpus of Latin literature](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/dco/article/view/116508){:target="_blank"}, by Ute Tischer and Susanne Schulze**, models invective speech in Catullus and other Latin poetry, calculating an "invectiveness" score for each poem from quantifiable textual markers.
* **[Coining AI: Roman Numismatics, Semantic Modeling, and Data Criticism in Academic Teaching](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/dco/article/view/115988){:target="_blank"}, by Johannes Wienand, Mattia Celisi, and Anna-Sophie Haake**, describes a teaching project in which students trained an AI to read the Latin legends on Roman coins, supported by a numismatic knowledge graph.
* **[Die Häufigkeitsverteilung von Wort- und Zeichenformen als Fingerabdruck eines Autors am Beispiel von Herodot und Thukydides](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/dco/article/view/115826){:target="_blank"}, by Hannes Kahl, Sylvia Kurowsky, Charlotte Schubert, and Jens Wittig**, presents a stylometric comparison of Herodotus and Thucydides based on the frequency distributions of word and character trigrams.
* **[Geo-Semantic Annotation for Ancient Textual Sources. Annotating Pliny the Elder's Description of Greece (4,1–74)](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/dco/article/view/115596){:target="_blank"}, by Laura Soffiantini**, presents a workflow for the geo-semantic annotation of place names in Pliny the Elder's description of Greece, including rare, disputed, and competing identifications.
* **[Light Linking: A Concept for Connecting Prosopographies](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/dco/article/view/115895){:target="_blank"}, by James Baillie**, proposes a lightweight framework for linking prosopographical databases, based on assertion graphs and web annotation models.

Additional publications of relevant to DANES from the past four months are:

* **[Artificial intelligence in zooarchaeology: Convolutional neural networks to classify duck bones](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2026.106608){:target="_blank"} (article, Journal of Archaeological Science), by Elisa Paperini, Beatrice Demarchi, Nevio Dubbini, Gabriele Gattiglia, Lisa Yeomans, and Claudia Sciuto**, presented a proof of concept for identifying duck bones and species from photographs with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), to support the reconstruction of Eastern Mediterranean environments at the Pleistocene–Holocene transition, with dataset and code on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20261868){:target="_blank"}.
* **[Classical Greek in the Open Greek and Latin Project](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.575){:target="_blank"} (article, Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Gregory Crane, Alison Babeu, Lisa Cerrato, Rhea Lesage, Leonard Muellner, Bruce Robertson, and Lucie Stylianopoulos**, traces nearly forty years of development of the open Classical Greek corpus of the [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/){:target="_blank"} and the First Thousand Years of Greek project. The collection now comprises 41 million words, of which 34.7 million in 1,946 texts carry chronological metadata from the 8th century BCE to the 13th century CE. 600 million words of Greek were extracted from Harvard's newly released OCR of nearly 1 million books, half of them texts previously unavailable in open form; the data are available on [GitHub](https://github.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/){:target="_blank"} and [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19581321){:target="_blank"}.
* **[Comparative Analysis of Machine and Deep Learning Methods for Cuneiform Text Classification](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-25620-1_31){:target="_blank"} (conference proceedings, ICAIN 2025), by Noor Walid Khalid, Saba Hussein Rashid, Noor Saud Abd, Ammar Farooq Abbas, Mustafa Abdulfattah Habeeb, and Yahya Layth Khaleel**, compared seven classical machine learning models for classifying 36,891 transliterated tablets from the [CuneiML](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.151){:target="_blank"} dataset (deprecated from [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/doi/10.5281/zenodo.8307503){:target="_blank"}) by language, period, and text type. Language and text-type labels were assigned through keyword matching on the transliterations. Their neural network model reached 99% accuracy for language, 96% for period, and 75% for text type.
* **[DDD – A Diagnostic Dataset for Character Recognition and Detection on Ancient Egyptian Hieratic Characters and Words](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-36033-5_26){:target="_blank"} (conference proceedings, ICDAR 2026), by Stephan M. Unter and Elena L. Hertel**, presented the Diagnostic Deir el-Medina Dataset (DDD): 17,885 polygon-annotated instances of individual signs, frequent sign groups, and words, in more than 500 classes from 50 hieratic papyri from Deir el-Medina (c. 1200–1070 BCE) in the Museo Egizio, Turin. The authors found that convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly ResNet50, clearly outperformed transformers and zero-shot feature extractors such as CLIP and DINOv3, with most errors arising between visually similar signs. Baselines for detection with YOLO and RT-DETR models showed that locating signs within full documents remains very difficult, especially for sign groups and words; the dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20553713){:target="_blank"}.
* **[Energy costs of Hannibal's alpine crossing](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2612764123){:target="_blank"} (article, PNAS), by Emilio Berti and Fritz Vollrath**, used energy-landscape modeling to estimate the direct energy costs for the men, horses, and 37 war elephants of Hannibal's army on four possible routes across the Alps in 218 BCE. The route via the Col de la Traversette was the shortest and least costly, while alternatives would have required 11–19% more energy. The authors estimate that the elephants would have used only about 4% of their body fat reserves, compared with 19% for the men, and discuss what this implies about Hannibal's logistical and tactical reasoning. GIS layers and code are available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19496894){:target="_blank"}.
* **[History and Artificial Intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009572187){:target="_blank"} (book, Cambridge Elements in Historical Theory and Practice), by Marnie Hughes-Warrington**, is an introduction for history students and teachers to the theory of history and artificial intelligence (AI), in four sections. They cover designing AI "artificial historians" trained on data from the past, how these can help historical research and education (such as dating and placing damaged Roman inscriptions), how they can cause harm through silences, bias, and appropriation, and the ethics, regulation, and geopolitics of AI and history.
* **[Institutionalization and Market Integration in the Archaic Greek Economy: Modelling Overseas Trade from the Distribution Pattern of Samian Transport Amphoras](https://doi.org/10.1093/pastj/gtag021){:target="_blank"} (article, Past & Present), by Michael Loy**, compared the archaeological distribution of Samian amphoras with agent-based models of maritime trade, arguing that a network of trade hubs from the Black Sea to Spain, rather than random drift, organized Mediterranean exchange by the end of the 8th century BCE.
* **[The Oxford Handbook of Digital Classical Studies](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780197835210.001.0001){:target="_blank"} (edited volume, Oxford University Press), edited by Elton Barker, Olympia Bobou, and Rubina Raja**, treats classical studies, archaeology, and philology as a single field, and surveys current digital tools and methods and their applications in research, teaching, and heritage across 63 chapters on contexts, data, methods, and case studies.
* **[Perseus and the Open-Source Ecosystem of Greco-Roman Studies](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.595){:target="_blank"} (article, Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Gregory Crane, Alison Babeu, and David A. Smith**, reviews how the open data of the [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/){:target="_blank"} has been reused and enhanced by other projects, and in some cases reintegrated into Perseus. The authors survey three areas: TEI XML texts enriched with speaker, dramatic, metrical, and quotation data ([DICES](https://db.dices.mta.ca/app/){:target="_blank"}, [DraCor](https://dracor.org/greek){:target="_blank"}, [SEDES](https://github.com/sasansom/sedes){:target="_blank"}, and [quotation detection](https://github.com/dasmiq/idi-cites){:target="_blank"} in Harvard's Institutional Books 1.0), morphologically annotated treebanks ([Diorisis](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.6187256.v1){:target="_blank"}, [GLAUx](https://github.com/alekkeersmaekers/glaux/tree/v2.1){:target="_blank"}, [Opera Graeca Adnotata](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.8158674){:target="_blank"}, [Opera Latina Adnotata](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15183688){:target="_blank"}, and the [Daphne](https://github.com/francescomambrini/Daphne){:target="_blank"} treebanks), and named entity data ([Recogito](https://recogito.pelagios.org/){:target="_blank"}, [ToposText](https://topostext.org/){:target="_blank"}).
* **[Synthetic Training Data Generation for 3D Cuneiform Sign Recognition](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-36033-5_7){:target="_blank"} (conference proceedings, ICDAR 2026), by Jan Philipp Bullenkamp, Florian Linsel, Lisa Wilhelmi, and Hubert Mara**, presented a pipeline that generates synthetic 3D cuneiform tablets with automatic labels for wedge segmentation and sign detection, and showed that synthetic data improves a [Myria3D](https://github.com/IGNF/myria3d){:target="_blank"}-based segmentation network trained on experimental tablets 3D-scanned after each wedge was imprinted. The code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/JanPhilipp-Bullenkamp/SynTab){:target="_blank"} and the scans on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20608307){:target="_blank"}.

## Special Mention

**[Digital Humanities 2026: Book of Abstracts](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21495909){:target="_blank"} (conference proceedings, Zenodo), edited by Song-yi Jung, Gayeon Kim, Seonyeong Park, Iro Lim, and Haein Ji**, collects more than 500 abstracts from DH2026, the annual conference of the Alliance of Digital Humanities Organizations (ADHO), held as a hybrid event in Daejeon, South Korea under the theme "Engagement." Around 20 contributions deal with the ancient Near East and Mediterranean, including a linked open data Elamite dictionary, an IIIF-based edition platform for cuneiform tablets, large language models (LLMs) for Egyptian–Coptic translation, computational paleography of Greek papyri, bias in digital epigraphy, and a [mini-conference session on AI and ancient scripts](https://ai4asconference.github.io/2026/index.html){:target="_blank"}.

**[Large language models can predict the results of social science experiments](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x){:target="_blank"} (article, Nature), by Ashwini Ashokkumar, Luke Hewitt, Isaias Ghezae, and Robb Willer**, used GPT-4 to simulate how representative samples of Americans would respond in 70 preregistered survey experiments, with 469 effects and 119,330 participants. The predicted effects correlated strongly with the actual ones, reaching the accuracy of pooled human forecasters, although they systematically overestimated effect sizes. This has interesting ramifications for AI's ability to correctly predict real human responses. Data and code are available on [Code Ocean](https://codeocean.com/capsule/9843791/tree/v1){:target="_blank"}.

**[A Theory-First Approach Towards Using Generative AI for Humanities Research](https://doi.org/10.1017/chr.2026.10039){:target="_blank"} (article, Computational Humanities Research), by Andrew Piper**, argues that humanities research with generative AI should be theory-first rather than tool-first, because prompts act as measurement instruments and small changes to them can alter conclusions. He proposes a three-step framework in which theory guides problem formation, prompt validation, and interpretation, with robustness testing across prompt variations.

**[Reimagining research papers as interactive and reliable AI agents](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11044-y){:target="_blank"} (article, Nature), by Jiacheng Miao, Joe R. Davis, Yaohui Zhang, Jonathan K. Pritchard, and James Zou**, introduced Paper2Agent, a framework that automatically turns a research paper and its code into an AI agent built on the [Model Context Protocol (MCP)](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/){:target="_blank"}, which reproduces the paper's results and answers new queries with validated tools. The code is available on [GitHub](https://github.com/jmiao24/Paper2Agent){:target="_blank"}.

**[Remote Sensing-Based Analysis of Archaeological Site Damage in Syria: Revisiting a Post-War Landscape](https://doi.org/10.3390/heritage9060209){:target="_blank"} (article, Heritage), by Jesse Casana, Jasper A. Clayton, Mary Lamberth, and Carolin Ferwerda**, used high-resolution satellite imagery to reassess damage at around 200 archaeological sites across Syria since the early years of the civil war, documenting a novel form of mechanized looting in the north, expanded militarization and refugee camps on prominent sites, and small-scale looting at 59% of the sites observed.

**[A Review of Artificial Intelligence Techniques in Oracle Bone Inscriptions](https://doi.org/10.1145/3837082){:target="_blank"} (article, ACM Journal on Computing and Cultural Heritage), by Jiaze Cai, Hengyi Li, Bang Li, and Lin Meng**, reviews artificial intelligence (AI) methods and datasets for Shang-dynasty oracle bone inscriptions, from duplicate detection and fragment rejoining to character recognition and decipherment support. The authors identify data scarcity, weak generalization across photographs, rubbings, and facsimiles, and the gap between visual matching and cultural interpretation as the main bottlenecks.

## Datasets Published

**[The 1909–1910 Gebelein Manuscript Inventory (Inventario Manoscritto): Archival Data from the M.A.I. Excavations](https://doi.org/10.5334/joad.220){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Archaeology Data), by Aneta Skalec**, presents a digital edition of Ernesto Schiaparelli's handwritten register of 1,906 artifacts from the Italian Archaeological Mission's 1909–1910 season at Gebelein, Egypt, which were transferred to the Museo Egizio in Turin and date from the Predynastic period to Late Antiquity. The dataset includes a diplomatic transcription of the Italian text, English translations, standardized object descriptions, and a comparison of each entry with the museum's collection database (SiME), organized by provenance area. It is available in the [RADOGOST repository](https://doi.org/10.82343/GGUZH5){:target="_blank"}.

**[Archaeozoology of the Bronze Age Herders: An Open Zooarchaeological Dataset from the South Urals and Northern Kazakhstan](https://doi.org/10.5334/joad.217){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Archaeology Data), by Alexey Rassadnikov**, presents data on 123,937 animal bones from 65 Bronze Age settlements, burial complexes, and mines in the Southern Urals and Northern Kazakhstan (c. 3500–1100 BCE), mostly from Sintashta and Alakul contexts. Analyzed over nearly two decades, the records cover species, skeletal element, fragmentation, measurements, surface modifications, pathologies, tooth wear, and X-ray age estimates for cattle metapodials. The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22714190){:target="_blank"}.

**[Conceptual Metaphors for Jealousy in Latin: A Corpus-Based Dataset](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.573){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Roberta Grazia Leotta**, presents 831 metaphorical uses of five Latin jealousy terms (*invidia*, *livor*, *aemulatio*, *obtrectatio*, and *malevolentia*), identified among 1,324 occurrences extracted from the *Antiquitas* section of the [Library of Latin Texts](https://about.brepolis.net/library-of-latin-texts/){:target="_blank"} (2nd century BCE–2nd century CE). The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19653015){:target="_blank"}.

**[C-Turkey: A Comprehensive Radiocarbon Dataset from Türkiye](https://doi.org/10.5334/joad.213){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Archaeology Data), by N. Ezgi Altınışık**, presents 3,390 radiocarbon dates from 59 provinces of Türkiye, spanning from the Epipalaeolithic (c. 22,000 cal BCE) to the Late Ottoman period, including 885 dates previously absent from global datasets. Skeletal samples are annotated by species and linked to ancient DNA identifiers. The dataset and R scripts are available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20011917){:target="_blank"}, with a searchable [Dataset Explorer](https://altinisik.github.io/C-Turkey){:target="_blank"} and a [calibration tool](https://altinisik.github.io/cturkey-app){:target="_blank"} online.

**[Emotion Vocabulary in Anatolian Languages: Etymology and Conceptual Structure](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.556){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Maria Molina and Ksenia Uvarova**, presents 72 etymological entries and 150 derivational nests (word families) documenting emotion vocabulary across seven Anatolian languages: Hittite, Luwian, Palaic, Lydian, Lycian A and B, and Carian. Compiled from the [eDiAna](https://www.ediana.gwi.uni-muenchen.de/){:target="_blank"} corpus and the [Hethitologie-Portal Mainz](https://hethport.net/HPM/index.php){:target="_blank"}, the entries group cognates by emotional domain with Proto-Anatolian and Proto-Indo-European reconstructions and semantic derivation patterns. The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19477826){:target="_blank"}.

**[Gazetteers of Latin Authors and Works for Chronological Modelling in the LiLa Knowledge Base](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.558){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Matteo Pellegrini, Francesco Mambrini, Giovanni Moretti, and Marco Passarotti**, presents gazetteers of 173 Latin authors and 539 works, from the 3rd century BCE to present-day ecclesiastical Latin, for the [LiLa](https://lila-erc.eu/){:target="_blank"} knowledge base. Each author is linked to Wikidata and CTS URN identifiers and dated with new start- and end-date properties. The gazetteers are available on [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/20038372){:target="_blank"}.

**[Geochemical Reference Data on Ancient Iron Industry from Ariège, Aude, Pyrénées-Orientales (Occitanie, France) and Andorra](https://doi.org/10.5334/joad.200){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Archaeology Data), by Gaspard Pagès and Alexandre Disser**, presents chemical analyses of smelting slag and iron ore collected through surveys and excavations in the eastern Pyrenees and the Montagne Noire, spanning from the late Roman Republic (late 2nd–1st century BCE) to 1876. The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17992515){:target="_blank"} and supports provenance studies of iron circulation in the ancient western Mediterranean.

**[Lead White in Context Across Greco-Roman Sources: The First TheSu XML Annotation Dataset of Arguments and Recipes, with Graph Visualisations and Discussion of their Design](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.568){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Daniele Morrone**, applies a new annotation schema to passages on lead white (ψιμύθιον/cerussa) in Plato, Theophrastus, Dioscorides, and Plutarch, alongside two modern studies replicating the ancient recipes. Arguments are mapped across sources, and recipes are broken down into their individual steps, which a Python pipeline converts into argumentation maps with [Graphviz](https://graphviz.org/){:target="_blank"} and network graphs with [Gephi](https://gephi.org/){:target="_blank"}. The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20290847){:target="_blank"}.

**[M/OTHERING_Gr: A Database of Terracotta Figurines Representing Adults with Subadults from Ancient Greece](https://doi.org/10.5334/joad.211){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Archaeology Data), by Giulia Pedrucci, Ricardo Fernandes, and Carlo Cocozza**, presents a database of 730 terracotta figurines depicting adults with children from mainland Greece, the Aegean islands, and Illyria, dating from about 700 BCE to 50 CE and found in sanctuaries, tombs, and other contexts. Compiled from publications, museum visits, and unpublished material identified with curators, the records combine iconographic, typological, contextual, chronological, and geospatial metadata, including caregiving interactions such as breastfeeding, eye contact, and body contact. The dataset is available on [Hebe](https://doi.org/10.48493/vswy-ks92){:target="_blank"}.

**[Open Digital Data on Funerary Landscapes and Settlement Organisation in Pre-Roman Apulia: Monte Sannace and Vaste](https://doi.org/10.5334/joad.209){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Archaeology Data), by Dominik Hagmann and Matthias Hoernes**, presents a dataset of 379 funerary features, representing about 474 buried individuals, mainly from the late Classical and early Hellenistic periods (4th–3rd centuries BCE). Compiled from published literature and earlier datasets and mapped in QGIS, it includes spatial, chronological, depositional, and burial information in CSV and KML formats, with data dictionaries and bibliographies, and is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20451742){:target="_blank"}.

**[The Palatine Anthology – Book 1: A Metrical and Prosodic Statistical Study](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.561){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Humanities Data), by Simona Nicolae, Cristian Șimon, and Ioan-Andrei Nicolae**, presents a metrical and prosodic analysis of the 123 Greek epigrams (500 lines) in Book 1 of the Palatine Anthology, composed between the 5th and 10th centuries in hexameter, elegiac, and iambic verse. It records syllable quantity, metrical feet, caesurae, diaereses, bridge violations, and the position of the accent, and shows a transition from quantitative to stress-based versification. The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19445316){:target="_blank"}, with an interactive HTML viewer whose code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/Vexiona/metrical-viewer){:target="_blank"}.

**[T'OMIM: A Morphologically Annotated Dataset of Parallel Passages in the Hebrew Bible](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.608){:target="_blank"} (Journal of Open Humanities Data), by David M. Smiley**, presents 554 narrative parallels between Chronicles and Samuel–Kings and 256 poetic parallels, drawn from published scholarly catalogs and aligned at verse and word level to the ETCBC's [BHSA](https://github.com/ETCBC/bhsa){:target="_blank"} morphological database. The resulting tables contain 28,009 word-level rows with full morphological annotation, supporting the training and evaluation of models for semantic similarity and text reuse in Classical Hebrew. The dataset is available on [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19135730){:target="_blank"}.

### Call for Papers

**[NAACL 2027](https://2027.naacl.org/calls/main_conference_papers/){:target="_blank"} and [COLING 2027](https://2027.coling-iccl.org/){:target="_blank"}** share an [ACL Rolling Review (ARR)](https://aclrollingreview.org/dates){:target="_blank"} submission cycle for long (8-page) and short (4-page) papers. **NAACL 2027**, the conference of the Nations of the Americas Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), will take place on **June 1–5, 2027** in **San Francisco, USA**. Its theme track is "Language as a Medium for Agentic Communication," and its areas include machine translation, multilinguality and language diversity, and low-resource methods for natural language processing (NLP). **COLING 2027**, the 32nd International Conference on Computational Linguistics, will take place on **May 9–14, 2027** in **Macau, China**. Its special theme, "NLP for Linguistics," invites work that uses NLP and large language models (LLMs) to advance linguistic research, with a focus on linguistic diversity and under-resourced languages, and its topics include NLP for digital humanities and cultural analytics, language resources, morphology and syntax, and language evolution. **Submission deadline is October 12**.

**[Theorising Networks: Relational Frameworks in Archaeology and History](https://www.helsinki.fi/en/researchgroups/interdisciplinary-research-network-for-ancient-near-east/news/call-for-papers-theorising-networks-relational-frameworks-in-archaeology-and-history){:target="_blank"}** is a workshop organized by the Interdisciplinary Research Network for Ancient Near East that will take place on **March 18–19, 2027** at the **University of Helsinki, Finland** (in person), examining the theoretical and epistemological implications of network approaches as frameworks for understanding an interconnected ancient world. Abstracts (300 words max) should address the implications of the "relational turn" in archaeology and history, network analysis as a conceptual framework versus a methodological tool, or interdisciplinary engagement between network science, archaeology, and history. Limited travel and accommodation support is available for selected speakers in financial need. **Submission deadline is November 16**.

**[DH2027](https://dh2027.adho.org/){:target="_blank"}**, the annual conference of the Alliance of Digital Humanities Organizations (ADHO), will take place on **June 28–July 3, 2027** in **Galway, Ireland** (partly hybrid) under the theme "Creativity," and has opened its [call for proposals](https://dh2027.adho.org/call-for-proposals/){:target="_blank"} to work across the full range of digital humanities, including work that does not engage directly with the theme. Submissions may be long papers (2,500–3,000 words, published in the *Anthology of Computers and the Humanities*), short papers (1,000–1,250 words), posters (500–750 words), half-day workshops, or day-long mini-conferences, and may be designated for the Technical Track or the new Digital Art(s) Track; all submissions must be in English and are made through [ConfTool](https://www.conftool.pro/dh2027/){:target="_blank"}. **Submission deadline is November 20**.

**[Reading the Past with AI: Computational Approaches to Historical Artifacts](https://scai.sorbonne-universite.fr/news/call-for-papers){:target="_blank"}** will take place on **April 29–30, 2027** at **Sorbonne Université, Paris, France**. Topics include handwritten text recognition (HTR) and optical character recognition (OCR) and computational paleography; computational codicology; computational restoration of fragmentary manuscripts, papyri, and inscriptions; multispectral and hyperspectral analysis of pigments, inks, and supports; computer vision for seals, coins, and ceramics; and knowledge representation and entity extraction from epigraphic, papyrological, and codicological corpora, while digitization projects and digital editions are outside the scope. Proposals of up to 500 words with a brief biographies should be sent to [sophie.robert@sorbonne-universite.fr](mailto:sophie.robert@sorbonne-universite.fr) and [maria-victoria.eyharabide@sorbonne-universite.fr](mailto:maria-victoria.eyharabide@sorbonne-universite.fr), and the proceedings will be published as a peer-reviewed special issue of *Digital Medievalist*. **Submission deadline is December 1**.

**[DH Benelux 2027](https://2027.dhbenelux.org/call-for-papers/){:target="_blank"}** will take place on **July 6–9, 2027** at the **Université libre de Bruxelles, Brussels, Belgium**, as an in-person conference (pre-conference workshops on July 6), under the theme "Where Is Digital Humanities?", which examines the disciplinary identity, public credibility, and engagement with generative AI of digital scholarship after large language models (LLMs). Topics include digital humanities methodology and institutional futures, computational ethics, LLMs and AI systems, reproducibility, multilingual DH, digital archives, and pedagogy. Submissions may be oral presentations, panels, or workshops (1,000–1,500 words), demonstrations (500–750 words), or lightning talks and posters (about 500 words), with accepted authors invited to submit full articles to the *DH Benelux Journal*. **Submission deadline is December 7**.

**[ACL 2027](https://2027.aclweb.org/calls/main/){:target="_blank"}**, the 65th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), will take place on **August 17–22, 2027** in **Kyoto, Japan**, as a hybrid conference. Its theme track, "Homogenization and knowledge collapse in LLMs," invites empirical, theoretical, survey, and position papers on how training and alignment practices can reduce diversity in the outputs of large language models (LLMs), creating a "generative monoculture." Topics include metrics for homogenization, the mechanisms behind it, human versus model diversity, and uniformity across languages. The general areas include language diversity, multilingualism, machine translation, phonology and morphology, syntax, and linguistic theory. Long (8-page) and short (4-page) papers are submitted through [ACL Rolling Review (ARR)](https://aclrollingreview.org/dates){:target="_blank"}. **Submission deadline is January 4**.
